Crop water use, the one number for every field

Few fields are metered. One number can be estimated for every field, whatever its water source: how much water the crop used in the month. Plants give off water as they grow, and satellites can pick that up.

or a meter reading

Rain and canal water the crop could use are subtracted from crop water use first, and what is left is the remainder. What it is called depends on what the field has, in the next table.

What the remainder is called, by what the field has

Outcome A meter reading A well, no meter No well
After the subtraction Kept beside the reading for comparison; nothing written from it Groundwater consumed, and groundwater extracted after the division Water use recorded, no supply reported, never as pumping
What the groundwater allocation is charged The reading Groundwater extracted Nothing

Water use recorded, no supply reported says only that the supplies on record did not cover crop water use; it is never written as pumping, and it is never a finding about the grower.

A district with no wells

The same steps run; with no well, the remainder is water use recorded, no supply reported, and the well lines stay empty.

What the satellite estimate still does

Job Rule
1Sets each field's share of a headgate total In proportion to crop water use after rain, up to what each field could use.
2Works out each field's remainder Crop water use minus rain and canal water the crop could use.
3Estimates the delivery of a diversion with no meter From the fields it serves, capped at what is left of the water right's face value.

Why satellites

Most water diversions in California are not metered, and for most fields nobody knows the volume delivered. Satellite estimates of crop water use cover the fields no meter does. OpenET turns satellite imagery into a crop water use estimate for any field. This platform labels it as an estimate.
