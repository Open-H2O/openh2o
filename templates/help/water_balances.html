The short version

Few fields are metered. One number can be estimated for every field, whatever its water source: how much water the crop used in the month. Plants give off water as they grow, and satellites can pick that up. Every other figure on this platform is a supply, matched against that estimate.

What this demonstration cannot show

This demonstration has no delivery records for any field's own gate. Each headgate's monthly total is built from the crop water use after rain of the fields it serves, and the software then divides that total back among the same fields on the same basis. On a canal-served field here, canal water therefore covers all or nearly all of crop water use after rain (never less than 85% in this demonstration), and groundwater consumed is the small remainder. A district's own gate records would change that: a field with a recorded delivery of its own keeps it, and only what is left is divided among the other fields.

Three sources, and how each is known

Rain

Rain that fell on the field, and the share of it the crop could use.

Estimated from weather data for the area
Canal water

Water the district let onto the field from its canal.

Recorded by the district at the headgate, from a meter or the ditch tender's estimate, then divided up among the fields it serves
Well water

Water pumped from a well on the field.

Known only when the month has a meter reading on record

Rain and canal water are subtracted from crop water use first, and what is left is the remainder. Its name depends on the field: groundwater consumed where there is a well and no meter reading, and water use recorded, no supply reported where there is no well. No device measures it; it comes from the subtraction. The Methods page explains each step.

The ending depends on what the field has

Where the remainder goes

Only a field with a well has its remainder written as groundwater. On a field with no well, the remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported, and nothing else is written. A missing meter never becomes a pumping figure.

A month with a meter reading

The meter reading stands as groundwater extracted, and the allocation is charged the recorded reading. The calculation still runs and is kept beside the reading for comparison, but it writes no groundwater row and no groundwater extracted of its own.

A well, no meter reading

The remainder is groundwater consumed. Dividing it by the share of pumped groundwater the crop consumes gives groundwater extracted, which is what the allocation is charged.

No well

The remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported. This means only that the supplies on record did not account for crop water use; it says nothing about why. It is never written as pumping.

A district with no wells

The same steps run on every field. With no well, a field's remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported, and nothing is written as pumping, so the well lines on this page stay empty. The platform keeps the district's diversion and delivery records month by month against its water right. The satellite estimate still does three jobs. First, it sets each field's share of a headgate's total: when the total is less than the fields could use, it is divided in proportion to each field's crop water use after rain; when it covers every field, each field gets what it could use (its crop water use after rain divided by its irrigation efficiency) and the rest is recorded against the headgate. Second, each field's remainder is worked out from it. Third, it supplies the estimated delivery for a point of diversion with no meter, from the fields that point serves. The Methods page says how.

Why satellites

Most water diversions in California are not metered, and for most fields nobody knows the volume delivered. A satellite estimate of crop water use covers the fields no meter does. OpenET turns satellite imagery into a crop water use estimate for any field. It is a public-private effort led by NASA, the Desert Research Institute, the Environmental Defense Fund and Google Earth Engine. This platform labels it as an estimate.
