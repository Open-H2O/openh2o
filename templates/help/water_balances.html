The short version

Almost nobody meters a field. One number can be estimated for every field whatever its water source: how much water the crop used in the month. Plants give off water as they grow, and satellites can pick that up. Everything else on this platform is a supply, matched against that estimate.

What this demonstration cannot show

This demonstration has no record of each field's own gate. It makes each headgate's monthly total from the crop water use after rain of the fields it serves, and the software then divides that total back among the same fields the same way. So on a canal-served field here, the canal water covers all or nearly all of the crop's water use after rain (never less than 85% in this demonstration) by construction, and the well's share is the small remainder. A district's own gate records would replace that. A field with its own recorded delivery keeping it, with the split dividing only the remainder, is not built yet; it is next.

Three sources, and how each is known

Rain

Rain that fell on the field, and the share of it the crop could use.

Estimated from weather data for the area
Canal water

Water the district let onto the field from its canal.

Recorded by the district at the headgate, from a meter or the ditch tender's estimate, then divided up among the fields it serves
Well water

Water pumped from a well on the field.

Known only when the month has a meter reading on record

Rain and canal water come off the crop's water use first. What is left is the remainder, and what the remainder is called depends on the field: the well's share where there is a well and no meter reading, and water use recorded, no supply reported where there is no well. It is not read from anything; it is what is left. Which figures those are, and what they are called on every screen, is on the Methods page.

The ending depends on what the field has

Where the remainder goes

Only a field with a well gets its remainder written as groundwater. A field with no well has nothing to pump, so its remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported, and left at that. A missing meter never becomes a pumping figure on a field with no well.

A month with a meter reading

The reading stands as groundwater extracted, and the allocation is charged the reading as recorded. The arithmetic still runs and is kept beside it for comparison, but it writes no groundwater row and no extracted figure.

A well, no meter reading

The remainder is groundwater consumed. Divided by the share of pumped groundwater the crop consumes, it becomes groundwater extracted, and that is what the allocation is charged.

No well

The remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported. The figure says the arithmetic did not close from the supplies on record and says nothing about why. It is never written as pumping.

A district with no wells

The same steps run on every field. With no well, a field's remainder is written as water use recorded, no supply reported, and nothing is written as pumping, so the well lines on this page never fill. The platform keeps the district's diversion and delivery records, month by month, against its water right. The satellite estimate still does two jobs. It decides each field's share of a headgate's total: when a headgate's canal total is less than its fields could use, it is divided up among them in proportion to each field's crop water use after rain; when it covers every field, each field gets what it could use (its crop water use after rain divided by its irrigation efficiency) and the rest is recorded against the headgate. And the remainder on each field is worked out from it. Using it to estimate a diversion that has no meter is not built yet; the Methods page says what that will do.

Why satellites

Most water diversions in California are not metered, and for most fields the volume moved is unknown. A satellite estimate of crop water use covers the fields no meter does. OpenET, a public-private effort led by NASA, the Desert Research Institute, the Environmental Defense Fund and Google Earth Engine, turns satellite imagery into a crop water use estimate for any field. It is an estimate, not a reading, and this platform labels it as one.
